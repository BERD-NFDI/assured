Why is Training the Missing Piece in Sensitive Data Access? 💡

Sensitive data – health records, administrative registers, qualitative interviews touching on people's private lives – holds enormous value for research. But making it available safely is hard, and the people responsible for that work are telling us, clearly and consistently, what they need most: training.

That's the headline finding from a recent study by the CESSDA (Consortium of European Social Science Data Archives) Sensitive Data Working Group, chaired by ASSURED team member Deb Wiltshire. Between April and May 2025, the group surveyed 20 Service Providers (i.e., institutions that provide access to research data) across Europe to understand what stands in the way of sharing sensitive data, and what support would help.

What the survey found

The results were striking. 65% of respondents identified training as a core need, ahead of legal expertise or guidance (55%) and examples of best practice (45%).

Financial and human resources topped the list too, but training wasn't far behind – and it's notable that it outranked even legal guidance. Services aren't just short of rules to follow; they're short of the practical know-how to apply them.

Taking it further in Bergen

Those findings didn't stay on the page. At the CESSDA 50th Anniversary Conference in Bergen, Deb Wiltshire and Brian Kleiner (FORS, Switzerland) ran a workshop "Advancing Access to Sensitive Data", using a 'World Café' format to dig deeper into the themes the survey had surfaced:

  • Limited financial and human resources
  • Lack of technical solutions and infrastructure
  • Lack of know-how, experience, or skills
  • Concern about risk and liability
  • Demand from researchers and data owners

Across every table and every theme, one message kept coming back: training matters, and it matters for two distinct groups – researchers and staff – each with different gaps to fill.

Researchers need practical guidance

Participants were blunt about the difficulties researchers face. Sensitive data is hard to access, and once access is granted, many users simply don't know how to handle it responsibly. A recurring problem was that researchers struggle to even recognise what counts as sensitive data in the first place, let alone how to manage it correctly through a project's lifecycle. What came up again and again was a simple, practical request: a checklist. Something concrete that tells researchers what to do at each stage, rather than leaving them to interpret abstract principles on their own.

Staff need it just as much – and the stakes are high

Training isn't only a researcher problem. Service provider staff were just as clear that they need formal training and guidance on what their organisation is actually required to do. Without that, individual staff members are left carrying decisions that should be backed by organisational policy and process.

The consequences of getting it wrong loom large. As one participant put it, the words that came up most in their discussion were reputation and trust – and reputational damage from a sensitive data breach can outlast any legal penalty. That's a heavy responsibility to place on staff who haven't been given the tools to manage it.

And the risk landscape is shifting. Participants pointed to generative AI as a new and largely unaddressed challenge: once data is fed into an AI tool, it may be impossible to remove, and even datasets that were previously considered low risk now carry a higher risk profile as a result. Staff can't be expected to navigate that shift without support.

The takeaway

Put together, the survey and the Bergen workshop tell a consistent story. Services want to open up access to sensitive data. The demand from researchers is real, and so is the public value of that research. But limited resources, technical gaps, and above all a lack of skills and know-how are holding that work back, and the fear of what happens if something goes wrong is enough to make services cautious.

Training won't solve every barrier on its own. But it's the one intervention that speaks to almost every theme on that list at once: it builds the know-how services say they're missing, it gives researchers the checklist they're asking for, it reduces the risk of costly mistakes, and it helps staff and researchers alike keep pace with a fast-changing technical landscape.

Where ASSURED fits in

This is exactly the gap the ASSURED project was set up to close, under the banner "safe research by safe people." It's building a suite of e-learning modules and a certification scheme, and its two-track design maps directly onto the needs this survey identified.

For researchers, ASSURED offers short, self-paced, interactive modules – the practical, checklist-style guidance Bergen participants said was missing. Essential modules cover what every researcher needs to know, with optional modules for specific roles and datasets, so training stays relevant without becoming one-size-fits-all.

For Research Data Centers and their staff, ASSURED provides a way to certify users and build team expertise systematically, rather than leaving staff to interpret organisational responsibilities alone – directly addressing the "what do we have to do as an organisation" gap raised repeatedly in the workshop.

The certification model also speaks to the risk and reputation concerns raised in Bergen, giving services a defensible, consistent standard rather than ad hoc judgement calls. It's an approach already established in the UK; ASSURED is now adapting it for Germany and, in time, more of the CESSDA network – turning the training gap this survey uncovered into a concrete, shared solution.

Read more about CESSDA at cessda.eu, or explore ASSURED's training modules at assured-training.org/trainings.

Meet Dr. Frederic Gerdon

Handling sensitive data responsibly is becoming an essential part of research. Yet for many researchers, formal training in safe, legal and ethical data use remains limited. This is exactly the gap that Dr. Frederic Gerdon is helping to address as a new member of the ASSURED project at GESIS – Leibniz Institute for the Social Sciences.

Frederic brings a combination of skills that align closely with ASSURED’s mission. His background rests on three core areas: empirical research, teaching, and data ethics. Trained in quantitative methods, he has worked extensively with data throughout his academic career. This experience gives him a strong understanding of how sensitive data are produced, what makes them valuable, and why they require careful handling.

Alongside his research expertise, Frederic has a clear commitment to teaching. He has taught at both Bachelor’s and Master’s levels and has completed formal training in higher education and learning. For him, teaching is not just about delivering content, but about designing materials that people actively engage with. This perspective plays an important role in ASSURED, where the goal is not simply to inform researchers, but to support meaningful learning and fostering the motivation to keep engaging with the covered topics.

A third pillar of his work is a long-standing interest in data ethics and privacy. Since his Master’s studies, Frederic has been engaged with questions around privacy and responsible data use. He is also involved in collaborative efforts to integrate data ethics more systematically into university teaching, including work with researchers at LMU Munich. This focus feeds directly into ASSURED’s aim to promote the ethical use of sensitive data.

Within the project, Frederic is primarily involved in developing and reviewing training modules. These modules cover key topics such as data ethics, legal frameworks for data protection, and statistical disclosure control. They are designed to support researchers working with sensitive data, particularly in secure environments such as Trusted Research Environments.

The first of these modules is already in use, providing guidance for researchers accessing data through the Secure Data Center at GESIS. Further core modules are currently in development and will expand the training offering in the near future.

What motivates Frederic most is the potential impact of this work. While researchers generally care about ethical and legal considerations, these topics are often addressed only in the context of specific projects. ASSURED aims to change that by offering structured, accessible training that researchers can use proactively.

Looking ahead, Frederic is particularly interested in seeing the training adopted more widely, across research data centers and beyond. The longer-term vision is to make high-quality training in secure data use easier to access, more consistent across institutions, and ultimately more embedded in everyday research practice.
